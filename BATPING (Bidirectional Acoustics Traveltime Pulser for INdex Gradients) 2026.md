# Overview

Assessments from the first cycle of LSST data indicate a need to further address image degradation. In particular, while optomechanical factors are being accounted for with high levels of precision, dome and mirror seeing remains uncharacterized ([Urbach et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.71929/rubin/2570536)). More specifically, temperature fluctuations within the telescope enclosure have the potential to perturb the optical path, decreasing image quality in an effect known as dome seeing. Because the index of refraction of air is temperature-dependent, turbulent pockets of warmer and colder air alike cause refraction of incoming wavefronts. This translates to a broadening of the telescope’s point spread function (PSF), the size of the image in the focal plane that captures an ideal point source, like a star.

BATPING will allow us to characterize temperature fluctuations within the telescope enclosure that perturb the optical path, decreasing image quality. BATPING offers the advantage that the speed of sound is much more sensitive to variations in temperature and hence refractive index. Additionally, BATPING’s ultrasonic signals, even along the optical path, do not interfere with normal observations, nor do they rely on dome illumination.

My work documented on this page will track two subprojects: (I) construction of BATPING’s audio hardware components, and (II) development of a machine learning (ML) approach to optimization of the acoustic signal. 

# Hardware Setup

The project apparatus can be characterized as an ultrasonic thermometer--measuring environmental temperature based on deviations in the speed of an ultrasonic pulse across a set path. Such a pulse is supplied by piezeoelectric ultrasonic transducers, organized into a hexagonal array to optimize amplitude and directionality (via near-field destructive interference); the pulse is reflected and then measured by an ultrasonic mic, such as the Dodotronic ultramic\_384k\_evo. Cross-correlation between the original pulse and the reflected pulse gives the time lag.

**Theory**

Piezoelectric transducers convert **changes** in voltage to mechanical displacement. Why? Well the initial signal (driven by the AWG) is a square wave voltage. When the current carrying this signal arrives at the metal electrodes (A and B) surrounding the piezo crystal, they accumulate positive and negative charge respectively (in the same manner as a capacitor), which results in the changing of the atomic spacing within the lattice of the piezo crystal. The best way I can think about this is the following: applying an electric field to many-electron systems will change the minimum potential energy (and the ground states) of the electrons in the crystal. This polarization will change the positions of the atoms, sending a pressure wave originating at the transducer surface, propagating outward.

Even though we’re currently only using the piezo transducers in the speaker direction, they can also be mics! If the initial signal is an acoustic square wave (oscillation of air pressure through space) then it exerts an oscillating force on the transducer surface when it changes the pressure there. This causes the piezo crystal to alternate between compression and expansion. The crystal sits between two metal electrodes (A and B), which accumulate positive and negative charge respectively via the shifting of atoms towards either electrode face (is this similar to the mobile charges in a p-n junction diode??). Thus, differences in potential accumulate and an AC voltage signal propagates with a matching frequency. So, without changes in pressure, there is no mechanical vibration of the piezo crystal.

General piezoelectric transducer circuit diagram

Chain of interactions: 

electrical signal --\> E-field exerted between electrode faces --\> atomic displacement and crystal deformation (i.e. Strain) --\> mechanical signal 

air pressure oscillation --\> mechanical vibration of the transducer face --\>strain in piezo crystal --\> electrical signal

# Matched Filtering

One formatting of the goal of BATPING is to measure the line integral of temperature fluctuations along potential stellar optical paths. One method of accomplishing this goal is to perform matched filtering, which involves the construction of a** template waveform** (usually taken as the average of many piezo responses recorded in a short time interval). I then run a cross-correlation of this template with a signal recorded after some sort of perturbation to the system. Nominally, this perturbation would be a fluctuation in the temperature--if the matched filter is constructed in standard conditions, then the signal’s acoustic traveltime will differ measurably when the signal encounters fluctuations in the temperature, or when the temperature changes in the time between the template and signal recordings. **Without thermodynamic insulation of the hardware and acoustic path or precise environmental temperature control, I am simulating temperature fluctuations using a different type of physical perturbation to the signal, namely manual changes to the path length.** Moving the reflector x cm closer to the piezo array + mic is equivalent to a temperature change of y degrees Celsius.

## **Physical Setup and Recording**

The piezo array and microphone are positioned 1m away from a reflector (a flat glass surface for now, but will be replaced by a proper corner-cube reflector). Then, the Agilent Waveform Generator (AWG) drives an input waveform (initially a 10 V\_PP, 2-cycle gaussian envelope with a 40kHz driving frequency), at a pulse frequency of 50Hz (one waveform sent every 20ms). Each 20ms time window contains two dominant features (1) an initial peak corresponding to the mic pickup of the outgoing signal (this is a transverse pickup, so the strength of the signal is low), and (2) a second peak corresponding to the mic pickup of the reflection after having traveled 2m round trip. After 10 periods, there is enough data to construct a template with N=10. The 20ms time windows are averaged, and the template is stored as a .npz file.

Then, the reflector is moved 10cm closer to the piezo speaker and microphone, such that the round trip traveltime is now 1.8m. The same input waveform is driven by the AWG, and a 20ms 2-peak segment of data is collected. The signal is stored as a .npz file.

The following was adapted from my phys15c final project report
GCC-PHAT is a signal processing technique that allows determination of the time delay between two signals.  We use this method to analyze the sound recorded from an array of microphones to localize the source.
\\begin{equation}
\\bf{Derivation}
\\end{equation}

Consider the matched filter template time series, denoted by $f\_1(t)$ and the signal time series, denoted by $f\_2(t)$, where
\\begin{equation}
f\_2(t) = f\_1(t - \\tau\_{12}),
\\end{equation}
Both time series contain an incident signal (picked up transversely) and its reflection after traveling the acoustic path length. $\\tau\_{12}$ is the difference between the expected traveltime (given by the template) and the observed traveltime (measured via the signal).  Fourier transforming these time series:
\\begin{eqnarray}
F\_1(\\omega)  & = & \\frac{1}{2 \\pi} \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} f\_1(t) e^{-i \\omega t} \\, dt, \\\\
F\_2(\\omega)  & = &  \\frac{1}{2 \\pi} \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} f\_2(t) e^{-i \\omega t} \\, dt = \\frac{1}{2 \\pi} \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} f\_1(t-\\tau\_{12}) e^{-i \\omega t} \\, dt, \\\\
                      & = &  \\left( \\frac{1}{2 \\pi} \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} f\_1(t^\\prime) e^{-i \\omega t^\\prime} \\, dt \\right)  \\, e^{-i \\omega \\tau\_{12}} =  F\_1(\\omega)  \\, e^{-i \\omega \\tau\_{12}}.
\\end{eqnarray}
Now construct
\\begin{equation}
H(\\omega) = F\_2^\*(\\omega) F\_1(\\omega) = \\left| F\_1(\\omega) \\right|^2 e^{+i \\omega \\tau\_{12}},
\\end{equation}
and
\\begin{equation}
\\tilde{H}(\\omega) = \\frac{H(\\omega)}{\\left| H(\\omega) \\right|}.
\\end{equation}
Taking the inverse Fourier Transform of $\\tilde{H}(\\omega)$, we find
\\begin{eqnarray}
\\tilde{h}(t) & = & \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} \\frac{H(\\omega)}{\\left| H(\\omega) \\right|} e^{i \\omega t} \\, d \\omega, \\\\
                 & = & \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} e^{i \\omega \\tau\_{12}}   e^{i \\omega t} \\, d \\omega, \\\\
                 & = & \\int\_{-\\infty}^{+\\infty} e^{i \\omega \\left(t+ \\tau\_{12} \\right) } \\, d \\omega, \\\\
                 & = & \\delta(t+\\tau\_{12}).
\\end{eqnarray}
The cross-power spectrum is normalized by its magnitude in Eq.\~(5).  This weighting factors out the amplitude information contaminated by noise and keeps the phase information.  The result of the Inverse Fourier Transform in Eq.\~(9) is a Dirac Delta Function peaked at time shift between Microphone 1 and Microphone 2, $t = -\\tau\_{12}$.

### Limitations of the gaussian+compensation Parametrization

**optimized\_waveform\_v2** gave a 31% improvement in the FWHM of the Auto-Correlation Function (ACF) peak (443--\>307µs), and **optimized\_waveform\_v3 **gave a 38% improvement in the FWHM of the ACF peal (443--\>275µs). These gains are significant, but the marginal increase of 7% improvement with the extra 800 iteration tells me that Bayesian Optimization under this parametrization is meeting some barrier preventing improvement past \~40%. This barrier could potentially be the theoretical limit of the optimization--there is a case to be made that the ringdown of the piezo transducers is a physical effect that, without targeted inverse filtering, will always spread the energy of the signal. However, I think that a first-order contributor to this barrier is the current parametrization. Under the first three rounds of optimization (v1, v2, v3), the input waveform was modeled as a modifiable gaussian with K-many “compensation” functions:
\\begin{eqnarray}
f\_{\\mathbf{\\theta}}(t) = A \\exp\\Big\[ -\\frac{(t-t\_c)^2}{2\\sigma^2} \\Big\]\\cos{(2\\pi f\_0 t+ \\phi)} + \\sum\_{k=1}^Kc\_kg\_k(t),
\\end{eqnarray}
where $A$ is the amplitude, $t\_c$ is the pulse center, $\\sigma$ controls the envelope width, $f\_0$ is the carrier frequency (40kHz), $\\phi$ is the carrier phase, and $c\_k, g\_k$ are the compensation coefficients and functions.

These “compensation functions” were designed to be pre-pulse or post-pulse sinusoidal bursts at the carrier frequency, with the goal being to have extra coverage for the time intervals when the behavior of the piezo could be most nonlinear (i.e. before or after the pulse). So, each carrier function g\_k is either a pre-compensation or a post-compensation.

1. **Pre-compensation: **There were 3 half-cycle bursts placed at fixed intervals before the main pulse center, each taking the form amp \* sin(2π f\_c t\_burst) for its rectangular window.
2. **Post-compensation: **There were 5 half-cycle bursts placed at fixed intervals after the main pulse center, each taking the form amp \* exp(-i \* 0.3) \* sin(2π f\_c t\_burst). The exponential decay is included to mimic the ringdown.

The optimization waveforms made some use of these compensation functions. The amplitudes of the three pre-center bursts were \[-0.47, -0.40. -0.01\] (relative to the waveform maximum). The amplitudes of the five post-center bursts were \[-0.03, -0.20, 0.42, 0.41, 0.46\]. 


Due to the parametrization, *the compensation functions are the only source of deviation from a strict gaussian.*In this way, the first three rounds of optimization have been very restricting, leaving simple sinusoidal bursts (at pre-set positions) as the only means of deviating from the gaussian structure. This leaves an open question of how to parametrize the waveform to yield a more expansive optimization.

Here’s what Claude has to say:

> “The optimizer essentially discovered a crude form of mismatched filtering or sidelobe suppression - using out-of-phase components to destructively interfere with the autocorrelation sidelobes. This is the same principle behind Barker codes and NLFM, just discovered empirically within a more constrained parameterization.”

## Narrative on FWHM\_corr, σ\_TOF, SNR\_corr, and δ\_FWHM 

**Definitions:**

1. FWHM\_corr: the full width at half max of the time-of-flight peak in the auto/cross-correlation plot of a single period of pulse travel; it is calculated by identifying the arg\_max of the peak region, and then running a parabolic fit on \[arg\_max - 5, arg\_max + 5\] (i.e. spanning from the 5 points before the crude max to the five points after it); the point on the fitted parabola at which its slope is 0 is the returned FWHM\_corr
2. δ\_FWHM: the uncertainty placed on the FWHM\_corr determination by the fitting algorithm; originally, when a three-point parabolic interpolation was used, the system was fully constrained (#degrees of freedom = 0), and therefore there was no uncertainty to return; however, now, δ\_FWHM gives a meaningful statistical metric for the per-period peak determination that can be compared with between-period metrics, like σ\_TOF.
3. σ\_TOF: the standard deviation from the mean time-of-flight measurement, averaged over consecutive independent pulses

As a metric to compare different waveforms during the optimization process, I have been using FWHM\_corr to understand the distinct shape and theoretical narrowness that different waveforms can achieve. FWHM\_corr is a robust metric, and it serves a particular purpose; however, it is not a direct determinant of the temperature resolution achievable in BATPING’s final implementation. Alternatively, σ\_TOF is the metric that ultimately determines temperature sensitivity, and, unlike FWHM\_corr, it is sensitive to inter-measurement systematic and statistical error (more below).

**Why FWHM\_corr is right for its current usage:**

While in the end we would like to know the precision with which we can detect temperature fluctuations (i.e. σ\_TOF), the current objective is to design a waveform optimized for the piezoelectric transducer’s non-linear response that affords the most accurate TOF calculations under standard conditions. FWHM\_corr is a robust method of measuring waveform performance, because it is less influenced by the variation of statistical/systematic noise between successive pulses. σ\_TOF is fundamentally a statistic representing multiple pulses, and it comes with its own error that scales like 1/√N (σ\_{σ\_TOF} = σ\_TOF/√N). FWHM\_corr, alternatively, is a single-pulse measurement observed to have very little variation between pulses. An input waveform will reliably elicit the same piezo response behavior (with some variable noise introduced along the way by the waveform generator, wires, air and audio disturbance, microphone noise floor, etc). This means that the peaks in the autocorrelation are expected to look the same, even if the location of the peaks may vary according to σ\_TOF.

**Why FWHM\_corr is limited in predicting final temperature precision:**

The FWHMs of peaks in an autocorrelation plot measure correlation peak width under fixed conditions: same temperature, same path length, same environment. Because σ\_TOF measures between-measurement variation (rather than the uncertainty of the time-of-flight determination for a single ping), σ\_TOF depends jointly on FWHM\_corr and SNR:
\\begin{equation}
\\sigma\_{\\text{TOF}} \\approx \\frac{\\text{FWHM}}{2\\sqrt{\\text{SNR}\_{\\text{peak}}}}
\\end{equation}

A narrower peak with poor SNR can actually give a *worse* calculated σ\_TOF in the end, as compared to a wider peak with a high SNR. The chirp compression experiments below demonstrate this quantitatively: the most aggressively compensated waveforms achieved FWHM = 98µs (much sharper than the optimized Gaussian V3 at 250 µs)* but actually gave higher *σ\_TOF, *potentially because the compensation amplifies noise at the spectral edges as much as it does the signal. *While I expect FWHM (or rather FWHM/SNR) to track with σ\_TOF, it is very possible that an elevated noise floor meant that this trend gets drowned out by noise.

**insert figure here showing expected and measured FWHM/SNR vs σ\_TOF (with analysis)**

Regardless, the cleanest metric for final performance is σ\_TOF. For reference, σ\_TOF is converted to tempreature precision using:
\\begin{eqnarray}
\\sigma\_T &=& \\frac{\\sigma\_{\\text{TOF}}}{2L \\cdot \\frac{dc}{dT}/c^2}
\\\\
&=& \\frac{\\sigma\_{\\text{TOF}}}{2 \\times 1 \\, m\\times \\frac{0.6 \\, m/s/^\\circ C}{343^2 m^2/s^2}}
\\\\
&\\approx& \\frac{\\sigma\_{\\text{TOF}}}{10.2 \\, \\mu s \\cdot \\, ^\\circ C^{-1}}
\\end{eqnarray}

**This means that our target of 0.1 °*****C *****is achieved by a σ\_TOF \< 1.02 µs per ping.**

## LFM Chirped Waveforms

A linear frequency-modulated (LFM) chirp sweeps from f\_start to f\_stop over duration T, producing a nearly-flat input signal power spectrum over bandwidth B = f\_stop - f\_start. Professor Stubbs aptly described this as a band-limited version of a delta function’s power spectrum, *spread out in time**. ***While a delta function contains all frequencies simultaneously, the chirp contains them sequentially. Given that our initial motive was to use a waveform that illicited a delta function piezo response (by eliminating ringdown tail and narrowing the response body), I tested the delta spectrum-like linear chirp.

### **Chirp Cross-Correlation Peak Shape, and Expected FWHM**

An LFM Chirp with bandwidth B has an approximately flat power spectrum over \[f\_0 - B/2, f\_0 + B/2\]:
\\begin{equation}
|S(f)|^2 \\approx \\text{rect}\\left(\\frac{f-f\_0}{B}\\right)
\\end{equation}

 When the Time-Bandwidth product of the chirp is very large, then this approximation works very well. Take for example the power spectrum of this 10 us, 10 MHz chirp that I found online, which has TBP=100:

 The Wiener-Khinchin Theorem states that the power spectral density is equal to the Fourier Transform of the autocorrelation. This gives us a sense for the theoretical autocorrelation peak shape. Reframed, to get the autocorrelation shape y(τ), simply take IFT(PSD):
\\begin{equation}
y(\\tau) = \\text{IFT}\\left( |S(f)|^2 \\right) = \\text{IFT}\\left( \\text{rect}\\left(\\frac{f-f\_0}{B} \\right) \\right) = B \\cdot \\text{sinc}(B\\tau)
\\end{equation}

because the Fourier Transform of the sinc is a rectangular window, and vice versa. **So, we expect the chirped waveforms to have sinc-like correlation plots.**

From this, we can calculate the expected FWHM for a sinc-like correlation peak. We want to take the FWHM of the *power response*, not amplitude, of the correlation plot.  The half-max of sinc^2 is:

> sinc^2(Bτ) = 0.5 → sinc(Bτ)  \~ 0.707 → Bτ \~ 0.443
> 
> FWHM = 2 x 0.443 / B = 0.886 / B

So, we expect the -3dB power width of the matched filter output to be 0.886/B wide.

### Caveat in Chirp Power Spectrum

In reality, however, when TBP is smaller and when the chirped signal passes through the piezo array and is acted on by a linear operator, the output spectrum will not be perfectly rectangular. For example:

## Chirped Waveform Testing

Below is a guide to the different characteristics of the chirped waveform that were modified, and the corresponding keys for each waveform:

### Bandwidth/frequency range:

- "bw\_6kHz" -- chirp bandwidth = 6 kHz, centered symmetrically around resonance (f\_0 \~ 41.5 kHz). This is pretty narrowband.
- "bw\_20kHz" -- chirp bandwidth = 20 kHz (31.5-51.5 kHz). This is pretty wideband.
- "bw\_25kHz" -- chirp bandwidth = 25 kHz (28 - 54 kHz). This is the widest configuration.
- "hf\_m15dB\_asym" -- "high frequency asymmetric". The passband is chosen to include all frequencies above the -15dB cutoff. This results in a passband that is not symmetric about f\_0, because the piezo resonance (as measured by the transfer function during my piezo characterization) extends further on the low-frequency side.

### **Amplitude shaping:**

- "comp" -- chirp amplitude = 1/H(f), where H(f) is the transfer function measured during piezo characterization. Ideally, amplitude-compensated waveforms should have a flat acoustic output spectrum because they compensate for the fact that the piezo will resonate less well when you drive it off-resonance. This has a 10% floor, meaning that it will stop boosting frequencies when they are lower than the -20dB point in the transfer function.
- "comp\_light" -- same amplitude compensation as above, but with a 20% floor (max -14dB boost). This was supposed to be a less aggressive form of amplitude compensation.
- "diff" -- diffraction-compensated: applies an additional f/f\_0 factor on top of the 1/H(f). I did this because Professor Stubbs suggested that the weird "forked" peak shape we were seeing could have been because of diffraction determining the arrival time of each frequency's beam of signal.
- "flat" -- no amplitude compensation. Honestly, since it performs as well as the amplitude-compensated waveforms, maybe we should stick with it since it's so simple.
- "comp\_rect" -- same compensation as "comp" but without the default Tukey window (Tukey window is essentially just a sine build-up and build-down in amplitude at the start and end of the chirp)

### **Duration:**

- 2ms, 4ms -- chirp duration before dead time. Longer duration should mean a larger time-bandwidth product, which would produce higher gain and a sharper autocorrelation peak in theory, though I'm not observing this currently.

### Alpha Sweep (alpha\_aX.X\_bw\_dur):

- "a0.2" … "a0.8" -- Another method of lightening the amplitude compensation:  exponent α in H(f)^{−α}. α=0 is flat (no compensation), α=1 is full 1/H(f).
- "sm" -- small bandwidth (6kHz, 38.5-44.5 kHz)
- "md" -- medium bandwidth (12 kHz, 36-48 kHz)
- "lg" -- large bandwidth (20 kHz, 32-52 kHz)

## A Two-Template Matched Filtering Method: The Stubbs Pipeline

**The Stubbs pipeline**

- Record \~400 periods. The first 100 are template periods (periods that will be used to construct the templates), and the remaining are evaluation periods (periods that will be treated as signals in the matched filtering).
- Co-add masked versions of the first 100 pulses to create two templates: (1) the incident peak template is the co-adding of periods where the waveform has been masked around the incident pickup, and (2) the reflection peak template is the co-adding of periods where the waveform has been masked around the reflected signal. Proper construction of these templates required precise identification of each ping's period. With data where recording times were \>10s, this meant accounting for significant drift in Ultrasonic mic + Siglent Waveform Generator. First, a crude determination of peak location was performed by finding the global max in each expected period range. Seeing as I have re-oriented the microphone such that incident pickup and reflection signals are recorded with roughly equal amplitudes, global max could be in either peak. So, I set a peak-finding algorithm to search for peaks at ±TOF (±5.7ms) before and after t\_{global max}. After finding both peaks, I set the window to be 2ms before the first peak maximum. As mentioned above, this is only a crude period construction, because there will be jitter between the actual start of each period and when the incident pickup amplitude is maximized. So, for every template period, run a **cross-correlation with the first template period (per0)**. The large central peak lag (hopefully near 0, but not guaranteed to be exactly 0) will give the window correction needed to properly align the period with per0.
- Perform matched filtering with the two templates on all evaluation periods. Δτ = τ\_reflection - τ\_launch gives the differential TOF relative to the environmental conditions when the template was created.
